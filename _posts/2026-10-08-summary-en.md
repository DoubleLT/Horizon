## [LeCun-backed H-JEPA learns hierarchical world models end-to-end for visual planning](https://twitter.com/ylecun/status/2107918930270409060) ⭐️ 7.0/10

A new paper advised by Yann LeCun, titled "H-JEPA: End-to-End Learning of Hierarchical World Models for Visual Planning," introduces a method that stacks action-conditioned JEPAs so each level predicts farther ahead in its own latent space. LeCun shared the work on X, where co-authors described it as the first end-to-end learned hierarchical world model for long-horizon visual planning. Long-horizon planning is a core bottleneck for embodied AI and robotics, and H-JEPA offers a task-agnostic recipe that could make latent world models scale to more complex visual control tasks. As the latest entry in LeCun's JEPA line, it also signals the direction he believes should replace generative reconstruction-based world models. H-JEPA plans coarse-to-fine: higher levels propose latent subgoals and lower levels refine them into primitive actions, with each lower-level planner comparing predictions against the assigned subgoal in the higher level's latent space. The paper reports Visual AntMaze experiments where two- and three-level H-JEPA are compared against a flat LeWM baseline in planning success versus planner compute.

twitter · ylecun · Oct 7, 19:40

**Background**: JEPA, or Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture, is a self-supervised paradigm that learns by predicting abstract latent representations of inputs rather than reconstructing raw pixels or generating tokens. Yann LeCun has championed JEPA as an alternative to generative world models, and H-JEPA extends it by stacking predictors across timescales so each level plans in its own latent space and passes subgoals down to finer levels.

## [Berkeley AI unveils QF3, a fast flow RL method with filtered Q-gradients](https://twitter.com/berkeley_ai/status/2107703044645539929) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Berkeley AI researchers introduced QF3 (Fast Flow RL with Filtered Q-Gradients), an online off-policy reinforcement learning algorithm that trains flow policies using flow matching combined with the critic's action gradient, backpropagated through a one-step prediction of the flow's output. The method reportedly enables training humanoid locomotion and manipulation from scratch, fine-tuning vision-language-action (VLA) models, and achieving roughly 10x faster training than prior approaches. Flow-based policies have become a standard approach for robot learning from demonstrations, but reinforcement learning is still needed to improve them or learn them from scratch; QF3 makes this process dramatically faster and more practical. If the claimed speedups hold, it could accelerate progress in humanoid robotics and VLA fine-tuning, lowering the compute barrier for labs and companies working on generalist robot policies. QF3 is described as an online off-policy RL algorithm that combines flow matching with filtered Q-gradients, using a one-step prediction of the flow's output to backpropagate the critic's action gradient. The arXiv paper (2610.08789) claims around 10x faster training for humanoid locomotion and manipulation, though the tweet itself provides only a brief announcement without detailed benchmarks or limitations.

twitter · berkeley_ai · Oct 7, 05:22

**Background**: Flow matching is a generative modeling technique that learns a multi-step mapping from a simple distribution (such as Gaussian noise) to a complex target distribution, and it has become a popular policy class for robot behaviors learned from demonstrations. Vision-language-action (VLA) models are multimodal foundation models that take an image observation and a text instruction and directly output low-level robot actions, pioneered by Google DeepMind's RT-2 in 2023. Reinforcement learning is often used to improve such pre-trained policies beyond the quality of their demonstration data, but applying RL to flow policies efficiently has been a challenge.

## [Prepending '\n\nOkay' makes base LLMs match RL counterparts](https://twitter.com/berkeley_ai/status/2107702268472733865) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Researcher Sewon Min showed that simply prepending the string "\n\nOkay" to a response makes the Olmo base model match its RL-trained counterpart, with the same effect observed for Qwen3-14B. The finding was retweeted by Berkeley AI, highlighting that a trivial prompt prefix can close the gap between base and RLHF/RL models. This suggests that much of the behavioral difference attributed to RLHF or RL post-training may actually be a surface-level formatting or style shift rather than a genuine change in underlying capabilities. If true, it would challenge assumptions about how much alignment training improves model ability and could simplify how researchers evaluate base versus post-trained models. The trick is remarkably simple: prepending "\n\nOkay" to the model's output, rather than changing any weights or training procedure. The observation was made on the Olmo base model and Qwen3-14B, a 14-billion-parameter dense open-weight model from Alibaba's Qwen3 series released in April 2025.

twitter · berkeley_ai · Oct 7, 05:19

**Background**: RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) is a post-training technique that trains a reward model on human preferences and then uses reinforcement learning to optimize a language model toward those preferences. Base models are the raw pretrained checkpoints before any such alignment or instruction tuning. Olmo is AI2's fully open language model family, and Qwen3-14B is a widely used open-weight model, making them common testbeds for comparing base and post-trained behavior.

## [Stanford AI Lab shares Level-of-Token layout idea for pretrained DiTs](https://twitter.com/StanfordAILab/status/2107898046793322726) ⭐️ 6.0/10

Stanford AI Lab retweeted Gordon Wetzstein's post introducing a key research idea: generalizing the uniform token grid of pretrained Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) into a Level-of-Token layout, where each token is a rectangle rather than a fixed square patch. The tweet is truncated and provides only a high-level conceptual description without technical details, results, or a linked paper. If successful, this idea could make tokenization in diffusion transformers more flexible and adaptive, potentially improving efficiency and quality for image and video generation. It signals ongoing research into moving beyond the rigid uniform patch grids that current DiT-based models like Sora rely on. The core idea is to replace the uniform square token grid with a multiresolution layout in which tokens can take rectangular shapes, echoing the recently proposed Level-of-Token (LoT) Diffusion framework for adaptive and efficient generation. However, the tweet itself is truncated and offers no implementation details, benchmarks, or code.

twitter · StanfordAILab · Oct 7, 18:17

**Background**: Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) are a class of generative models that replace the traditional U-Net backbone with a transformer operating on latent image patches, as introduced in the 2022 paper 'Scalable Diffusion Models with Transformers' by Peebles and Xie. In standard DiTs, the latent image is divided into a fixed uniform grid of square patches, each treated as one token. The Level-of-Token (LoT) Diffusion framework proposes turning this into an explicit multiresolution token layout, allowing tokens of different sizes and shapes for more adaptive generation.
